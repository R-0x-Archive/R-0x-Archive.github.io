The Glen Urquhart Disconnect: Is Highland Council Too Big to Exist?

Following the Scottish Government's landmark public sector reform proposal on September 21, the future of the UK's largest local authority—Highland Council—is officially on the line.

For nearly thirty years, a single administrative body in Inverness has controlled one-third of Scotland's total land mass. While Inverness sees growth, outlying rural regions continue to grapple with severe depopulation, crumbling roads, and a profound sense of political isolation.

The core issue is structural. When power is centralized in one city, local communities lose their voice. First Minister John Swinney's recent acknowledgement that "some councils are too big to be local" has thrown the gates wide open for a total structural break-up.

The Local Friction Points

To understand the current political battle, we have to look past the dense government lingo and focus on how power actually functions here. Under the current status quo, Scotland is managed by 32 individual councils. While places like Dundee or Clackmannanshire are physically tiny, our own Highland Council is a geographical monster.

This creates an illusion of local control. You will frequently hear about regional sub-groups like the Inverness and Nairn Area Committee. However, these small assemblies do not hold any real purse strings. The heavy-duty infrastructure budgets, housing allocations, and macro funding strategies remain tightly locked behind the main doors on Glenurquhart Road.

The Two-Tier Gamble

The newly proposed framework seeks to replace these 32 councils with a two-tier model: a small handful of massive Regional Authorities tasked with major strategic operations, sitting directly above roughly 120 to 160 hyper-local Community Authorities built around individual towns.

For Inverness, this is a distinct double-edged sword. While a dedicated local Community Authority might finally give our immediate neighborhoods genuine decision-making power over local high streets and planning laws, the overarching Regional Authority would cover an even larger territory than before—threatening to distance macro-decisions even further from the community's grasp.
