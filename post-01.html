The Glenurquhart Disconnect: Is Highland Council Too Big to Exist?

Following the Scottish Government's landmark public sector reform proposal on September 21, the future of the UK's largest local authority—Highland Council—is officially on the line.

For nearly thirty years, a single administrative body in Inverness has controlled one-third of Scotland's total land mass. While Inverness sees growth, outlying rural regions continue to grapple with severe depopulation, crumbling roads, and a profound sense of political isolation.

The core issue is structural. When power is centralized in one city, local communities lose their voice. First Minister John Swinney's recent acknowledgement that "some councils are too big to be local" has thrown the gates wide open for a total structural break-up.

Simplifying the Mega-Council Shuffle

To understand the current political battle, we have to look at how Scotland is chopped up right now compared to what the government wants to do next:

  • The Status Quo: Scotland is run by 32 individual councils. Some are tiny city-councils, but our Highland Council is an absolute monster covering a third of the country.
  • The Illusion of Local Power: You might hear about local sub-committees like the Inverness and Nairn Area Committee. While these sound local, they do not hold the real purse strings. The massive, strategic money decisions are locked inside the central headquarters on Glenurquhart Road.
  • The New Two-Tier Plan: The government wants to scrap the 32 councils entirely. They plan to replace them with roughly 6 to 10 giant Regional Authorities for heavy-duty infrastructure, and 120 to 160 tiny, hyper-local Community Authorities for individual towns.

For Inverness and the wider Highlands, this is a double-edged sword. While it could finally give local neighborhoods actual decision-making powers over their own high streets, it also threatens to absorb the wider region into an even larger, more distant super-regional authority.
